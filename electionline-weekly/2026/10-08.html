In Focus This Week

Countdown to November 3
Election reform ballot measures

By M. Mindy Moretti
electionline.org

Voting on voting has grown in recent years and this year is no exception. Voters in more than 21 states will vote on elections-related ballot measures both at the state and local level. 

Overall, according to Ballotpedia, there are 164 statewide ballot measures that are certified for the ballot in 40 states for elections in 2026. On November 3, voters will decide 145 (88%) of them. The remaining 19 were spread across earlier election dates. From 2010 to 2024, the average number of statewide ballot measures in an even-numbered year was 161, including 53 citizen-initiated measures.

Of the elections-related ballot measures, voter ID requirements and citizenship requirements certainly lead the pack, but there are also measures on election dates, recalls, special elections, ranked choice voting (to implement and repeal), and there are a number of ballot measures on ballot measures themselves. 

Here is a brief look at all the elections-related ballot measures at the state and local level that we found. If we missed one, please let us know and we’ll update this list. Special thanks to Ballotpedia for doing the heavy lifting on the statewide measures. 

Statewide Ballot Measures

Alabama: Amendment 1 – Require the office of lieutenant governor to be filled by election if a vacancy occurs more than 60 days before the next general election.

Alaska: Ballot Measure 2 – Repeal the top-four ranked-choice voting (RCV) system that was adopted in 2020.

Ballot Measure 3 – Amend state law to provide that “Only a person who is a citizen of the United States” may vote in any election

Arizona: Proposition 144 – Make a number of changes to the state’s election laws, including specifying that only citizens may vote in any Arizona election and requiring voters to show a government-issued ID to cast a ballot.

Arkansas: Issue 1– Provide that “Only a citizen of the United States … may vote in an election in this state”

California: Proposition 39 – Require voters to present government-issued identification when casting ballots and require election officials to use government data to confirm voter citizenship and report verification rates.

Proposition 5 – Eliminate the successor election when a state officer is recalled, thereby leaving the office vacant until it is filled according to state law.

Colorado: Amendment 84 – Require voters casting ballots by mail to place their signature and some form of real ID on their ballot.

Amendment 85 – Require ballot questions to be in plain language, at no more than an eighth-grade reading level.

Kansas: Citizenship Voting Requirement Amendment – Amend the state constitution to state that only a citizen of the United States is eligible to vote.

Massachusetts: Question 3 – Eliminate political party primaries for state elections and create a single primary where candidates are listed regardless of their political party.

Question 4 – Provide for same-day voter registration. 

Missouri: Amendment 6 – Prohibit the legislature from repealing or amending a citizen initiative unless at least 80% of both the House and Senate vote to refer a proposed change to the ballot, and voters then approve the change, prohibit the legislature from making changes to the citizen initiative and referendum process, among other changes.

Montana: CI-133 – Add the power of initiative and referendum to the state’s Declaration of Rights.

Nevada: Question 7 – Require voters to present photo identification when voting in person or to provide the last four digits of their driver’s license or Social Security number when voting by mail.

New Mexico: Constitutional Amendment 2 – Repeal the constitutional provision requiring that school elections be held at different times from partisan elections.

North Carolina: Require Voter Identification Amendment – Require photographic identification to vote for all voters, not just those voting in person.

North Dakota: Constitutional Measure One – Require constitutional amendments to receive a 60% supermajority vote to be adopted.

Ohio: Issue 3 – Require photo ID to vote.

South Dakota: Constitutional Amendment J – Provide that “[an] individual who is not a citizen of the United States is disqualified from voting in any election or upon any question submitted to the voters”.

Constitutional Amendment L – Require a 60% supermajority by voters to approve constitutional amendments.

Utah: Constitutional Amendment A – Require that constitutional amendments appearing on the ballot must be published for a period of 60 days.

Virginia: Question 3 – Provide that persons convicted of felonies have their voting rights restored upon release from incarceration

West Virginia: Citizenship Voting Requirement Amendment – Amend the constitution to state that only citizens may vote in elections.

Local Ballot Measures

Fairbanks North Star Borough, Alaska: This week the residents of the Fairbanks North Star Borough voted on two elections-related ballot measures. While ballots are still being tabulated it seems that Proposition 2, which would move municipal elections from from the first Tuesday in October to the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November; adjust the deadline for receiving mailed absentee ballots to account for the Veterans Day holiday; and change the terms of the Presiding Officer and Deputy Presiding Officer has been approved by about 55%. Proposition 3 called for amending the borough’s code amended to require that all ballots cast in Borough elections be hand counted by registered Borough voters and to prohibit the use of electronic tabulation systems to produce or certify election results; provide for observation of the hand-counting process as authorized by law; and become effective in 2027 and it failed by receiving only about 45% of the vote. 

Longmont, Colorado: On July 14, the Longmont City Council voted 6-1 to put a ranked choice voting (RCV) measure on the November 2026 ballot. If the measure passes, RCV will be used in Longmont elections for mayor and City Council beginning in 2029.  Pending release of technical guidance from the Colorado Secretary of State’s Office, Longmont would also implement multi-winner RCV – a form of proportional representation that ensures nearly all voters elect candidates of their choice. 

Honolulu, Hawai’i: On July 20, the Honolulu Charter Review Commission voted 10-3 to refer a ranked choice voting (RCV) measure to the November ballot. If the measure passes, RCV would be used to elect Honolulu’s mayor, City Council, and prosecuting attorney beginning in 2032. Currently, Honolulu uses two-round nonpartisan elections. All candidates compete in a spring primary, and the top two candidates from the primary advance to the general election. However, if one candidate receives 50% or more of the primary vote, they are elected and the general election is cancelled. That means races are frequently decided in the primary, when turnout is lower and less representative. 

Frankfort Township, Illinois: Voters in Frankfort Township will see an advisory question about voter ID laws on the General Election ballot. The exact question on the ballot will be: “Shall the State of Illinois enact a law to require a person to present a valid government­ issued photo identification card or passport establishing proof of their United States citizenship before allowing that person to vote in any Illinois election?” According to The Patch, Frankfort Township is one of several townships in Will County that is asking about voter ID laws. Green Garden Township and Crete Township have also placed similar questions on the ballot. Illinois does not currently require any ID when you go to the polls to vote.

Madison County, Illinois: Madison County voters will be asked on November’s ballot if they think Illinois should require identification to vote in elections. The county board recently voted 17-2 to place a nonbinding referendum on the ballot. While supporters of the advisory referendum say it’s a way to measure voter sentiment on a timely issue, critics of the referendum equate the question to President Donald Trump’s SAVE America Act. The question to Madison County voters reads: “Shall the Illinois General Assembly pass legislation to require government-issued identification to vote?” Even if the referendum passes in November, Illinois’ legislature, controlled by Democratic supermajorities, will not likely pass such a measure. In 2024, 56.5% of Madison County voters approved an advisory referendum that asked them about splitting from the Chicago area and forming a new state.

East Lansing, Michigan: A group of East Lansing residents submitted a petition to the City Clerk’s Office to place a proposal on the November ballot that would move City Council elections to even-numbered years, a change supporters say would boost voter turnout. If the effort is successful, it would have a profound impact on local elections, as far fewer people vote in odd-year elections, when City Council elections may be the only race on the ballot. In the November 2025 City Council election, about 6,300 ballots were cast, compared to more than 17,500 votes cast in the November 2024 election, according to data on the Ingham County Clerk’s Office. 

Le Grande, Oregon: Residents will not only cast their votes for city council and mayor in November, but also decide the future of elections in the city. If supported by voters, the ballot measure would create three revisions to the charter. The proposed amendment would eliminate the council position numbers. Instead of voting by position number, residents would select their top three candidates to serve on the council out of the entire pool of people running. The second proposed change would eliminate the language requiring a primary election. Currently if three or more candidates file for any position, the city holds the primary election and then the top two candidates advance to the general election. Finally, the proposal would allow the council to fill midterm vacancies through appointment for the remainder of the term.

The initiative is based on a similar effort clerks from Idaho tried several years ago, according to Weber County Clerk Ricky Hatch. “We’re always looking for ways to communicate how elections are run to the voters,” he told KSL’s “Inside Sources.” “We like what they did. In Florida, Local News 10 hosted a roundtable with elections officials from Broward, Miami Dade and Monroe counties to talk about what to expect during the next month. In Georgia, election officials from three coastal counties–Chatham, Glynn and Liberty–participated in a free community forum to answer questions about how elections are administered and protected.

Election Office News: Usually we lump a bunch of election office moves together in one round-up but given how long it’s taken the Mahoning County, Ohio Board of Elections to settle on a new office space, we felt they deserved their own space! The BOE will move to a former elementary school and officials expect to move in before the November 2027 general election. “About 80% of Mahoning County’s voters are within a 20-minute drive to this facility, and it’s also on a WRTA line,” said Denise Bayer, a board of elections member. The BOE has been seeking a new home since 2022, citing the deteriorating condition of its current building and the desire for a larger space. Numerous other locations were considered including a former Bottom Dollar store and the new government center in downtown Youngstown. The Western Reserve Port Authority and the Youngstown City School District agreed for the district to sell the former school building to WRPA for $900,000. Anthony Trevena, WRPA executive director, said that when the county commissioners notified the board of elections members they had the right to look at opportunities to relocate, they reached out to WRPA. For elections, the building needs to be scalable to accommodate nearly 200 parking spaces and a lot of traffic, he said. It also needs ample storage space for voting equipment and to be accessible geographically.  “When you look at all the properties available in this circumference, this just really did do a great job of checking all of those boxes,” Trevena said. “We definitely wanted to keep the board of elections in the city because it’s the county seat, and we’re also cognizant of not wanting to disenfranchise voters,” Bayer said. 

New Technology: Several jurisdictions have launched new technology for voters this election cycle. In Montgomery County, Pennsylvania it’s Mo, an AI chatbot created to answer basic election questions. Mo is a black-and-white, clickable, robotic dog seated on the bottom-right corner of the Montgomery County voter services website. The generative AI chatbot only relies on verified information from the voter services website and the county geographic information system, according to Montgomery County. From locating a polling location to instructions on how to submit a mail ballot, Mo has the 24/7 capability to spit back an answer and translate it into more than 70 languages. Montgomery County created Mo with the aid of Citibot, a Charleston, South Carolina-based software company that specializes in crafting chatbots for local governments. “Given all the disinformation that’s out there, we wanted to compete and be able to get accurate information out into the world and the way to do that is to use AI for public good,” Commissioner Neil Makhija said. Just don’t ask Mo who to vote for. “I’m not able to help with candidate recommendations or voting advice — that falls outside what I’m here for!” is the answer Mo types out when asked according to WHYY. Harris County, Texas recently launched the Harris Votes app that brings several of the county’s most-used election resources into one place. Voters can check their registration status, locate Vote Centers, review a personalized sample ballot, track key election dates and receive official reminders and updates. In a county as sprawling and diverse as Harris County, that kind of convenience is not merely high-tech; it is good public service. “We know voters want election information that is easy to access,” Harris County Clerk Teneshia Hudspeth said. “The Harris Votes app brings several of those resources together in one place, giving voters another convenient way to prepare for Election Day.”

New Research & Resources

De-Escalation: A Guide for Election Officials: Election officials regularly interact with voters, election observers, members of the public, the media, and others who may arrive to them and their offices with questions, frustrations, or strongly held concerns. From disputes over no-campaign zones at voting locations to First Amendment auditors visiting election offices, potentially contentious interactions can arise in many election settings. The Committee for Safe and Secure Elections’ De-Escalation: A Guide for Election Officials provides practical techniques election officials and their teams can use to help restore calm, reduce tension, and prevent difficult interactions from escalating any further. At the center of the resource is the CLARA Framework: Calm, Listen, Affirm, Respond, Address Alternatives. The framework begins with managing your own stress response before engaging with someone who is upset. From there, it emphasizes active listening, finding points of agreement or empathy, providing clear factual information, and offering constructive next steps when an individual’s concerns cannot be fully resolved. The resource also provides actionable guidance on body language, tone of voice, changing the setting of an interaction, and “tapping in and tapping out”—allowing another staff member to step into a difficult conversation and potentially reset its tone. Effective election de-escalation requires more than knowing what to do in the moment. Preparation and practice matter. Election offices should identify likely scenarios, establish clear protocols for when to de-escalate or involve security or law enforcement, and regularly practice realistic situations with frontline staff and poll workers.— Committee for Safe and Secure Elections

What if guide template: Provisional voting requires poll workers to navigate a series of decisions while also explaining the process to a voter who may already be confused or concerned about why they cannot vote normally. This What if guide template turns complex processes into a clear path, helping poll workers understand what to do and what to say. The template will help you create a short printable job aid where every provisional voting scenario gets: flow charts to help poll workers decide what to do next and scripts for poll workers to follow so they can speak confidently. It’s part of our new provisional voting toolkit, which also has sample social media messages for voters. In early October, we’ll add more tools to the toolkit including: Provisional form checklist job aid; and Take home notice template.— Center for Civic Design

Evaluating How the USCIS SAVE System Performs in Practice: Evidence from Election Offices: In May 2026, Utah reported the results of a “citizenship audit” of its voter registration rolls that it conducted in April. Of more than 2 million voter records analyzed, just 27 were found to be noncitizens, with another 25 likely noncitizens. The audit’s findings are noteworthy because they confirm what previous studies have consistently shown: that noncitizen voting, which is already illegal in every state for federal elections, is extremely rare. But the findings are notable for another reason too: they were made possible by a resource—the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) service, a program run by the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS)—that was only recently made available to election officials. After USCIS made a series of changes to SAVE last year, election offices in states across the country began adopting the tool with the purpose of identifying and removing noncitizens from voter registration lists (and preventing noncitizens from registering to vote in the first place). Until a federal court’s ruling in League of Women Voters v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security in June 2026, which temporarily blocked the 2025 SAVE expansions (a September 25, 2026 Supreme Court ruling allowed them to resume), many states were using SAVE results to inform decisions about who can register—and who stays registered—to vote. These decisions were affecting millions of voters and potential voters. Yet little is known about how accurate SAVE is, or the administrative burden that it created for officials. Our research project is an attempt to fill that gap.— Bipartisan Policy Center. 

What Voters Think Ahead of the 2026 Midterms: Election Information, Confidence, and Concerns: As voters prepare to cast their ballots in the 2026 midterm elections, the Bipartisan Policy Center worked with Morning Consult to survey 3,144 registered voters between September 2 and 4. Building on similar surveys fielded in 2022 and 2024,1 our 2026 survey questioned respondents about: 

  • Where they look for election information 
  • How confident they are in the vote-counting process 
  • Their concerns about the 2026 midterm elections 

We found that voters continue to look to local election officials for information about how to register and vote and about how elections are run. However, voters most commonly turn to online search engines for this information. Furthermore, our survey shows that confidence in the vote-counting process remains high, with a narrowing of the partisan gap in confidence that existed in 2022 and 2024. Regarding concerns about the 2026 midterm elections, Democrats and Republicans disagree on what is most concerning. However, both groups worry about the potential for misleading information—especially information generated by artificial intelligence—to disrupt the elections. — Bipartisan Policy Center

Chatbots Still Bad at Spotting AI-Generated Images Despite New Law: Author Abdiaziz Ahmed details how some popular AI chatbots cannot reliably detect AI-generated images depicting false incidents of election-related malfeasance even after the implementation of the California AI Transparency Act. The law requires tech companies to embed origin data in AI-generated images, video, and audio; provide consumers with tools to access the origin data; and give users an option to include a visible indicator that content is AI-generated. After rerunning some tests from an earlier Brennan Center study, authors found that most of the chatbots didn’t mention the embedded origin data and still appeared to largely rely on often-faulty visual analyses of the images. — The Brennan Center

Ballot Measures, Legislation & Rulemaking

Federal Legislation: Minnesota’s Republican congressional delegation is calling for a new federal reporting requirement on voter vouching, a ballot casting method that has been part of Minnesota elections for more than 50 years. Voter vouching allows a non-registered individual to bring a registered voter with them to their polling site to verify they live within that precinct. The registered voter vouches for the unregistered person. The delegation introduced the Voter Vouching Transparency Act, which would require states to provide standardized information after each general election. The bill’s reporting requirements include: The number of people who registered by voter vouching; The number of people who served as voter vouchers; The number of people vouched for by each voter voucher; and The number of registration attempts that were rejected through voter vouching.

Marion County, Indiana: The Marion County Election Board approved a new protocol for poll workers if federal agents, like immigration enforcement, the FBI or other law enforcement, show up at the polls. The policy states that if agents appear at or near a vote center polling location, workers are to note any details about what agency they are from, if they are armed and what time it is. They should then immediately call the election board or an inspector hotline if it occurs on election day. They also should not obstruct or physically block any officers and do not volunteer any information. Poll workers are also checking credentials for other permissible individuals who are allowed to be in voting centers, like election watchers, challengers, pollbook holders and the media. Marion County Clerk Kate Sweeney Bell said that while they believe this is an extremely unlikely scenario, they want to be prepared for anything. “This is to help reassure not only voters, but poll workers, that we also see what’s going on in the world, and we have a plan in case things go nutty on election day,” Sweeny Bell said. Marion County Election Board Director Patrick Becker said that he knows poll workers will likely have questions about these concerns. “It’s about us being able to tell them, we hear them too, and we have a plan on how to respond to those things,” Becker told WFYI.

Michigan: Michigan Sen. Chedrick Greene (D-Saginaw) presented a bill to the state Senate Elections and Ethics Committee that would require Michigan to reimburse county, city and township governments for the cost of conducting a special election.  The legislation, Senate Bill 1186, would cost the state an estimated $4,500 per voting precinct in a given special election based on reimbursements made to local governments for the 2024 presidential primary election, according to a Senate Fiscal Agency analysis of the bill. Sen. Paul Wojno (D-Warren), expressed his support for the legislation during Thursday’s hearing. “Being a former municipal clerk, I think every clerk around the state would be supportive of this legislation,” Wojno said. Greene clarified that the bill does not cover any election date where there are already scheduled elections for ballot proposals, bond proposals for schools or any other vote occurring. “If there’s any local municipality that is having a special election for once again recall of a trustee or something to that effect, they cannot submit to the state for the purpose of a special election reimbursement in this bill,” Greene said. “It is only for single issue state legislative purposes that are in the jurisdiction of the executive branch of the state of Michigan.”

Legal Updates

Federal Litigation: The Democratic National Committee (DNC) has filed a lawsuit alleging that the Trump Administration broke the law when it pushed through a last-minute change to the form overseas citizens use to register and request absentee ballots. The lawsuit contends that the Department of Defense made the change by declaring an emergency, which allowed it to eliminate a specific box on the form. That box is typically checked by potential voters who acknowledge they are U.S. citizens but who have never lived in the United States. They are usually the children of U.S. citizens, and 38 states allow them to cast ballots provided they have close family connections. Since 2013, the box has appeared on postcards sent to overseas voters. Republicans have filed lawsuits in multiple states trying to prevent those voters, who typically support Democrats, from casting ballots. Thursday’s lawsuit contends that the administration simply made the change on its own without giving proper notice. “These American citizens pay U.S. federal and state taxes, but now a years-old plan has culminated in a manufactured ‘emergency’ to justify impeding their right to vote in American elections and potentially disenfranchising them entirely,” the lawsuit states.

The Trump administration has been sued over its plans to deploy “a historic number” of election monitors to polling stations during next month’s midterm elections. The bid “stands to jeopardize” the elections and could “interfere with the right of voters to participate in the election free from intimidation”, according to the NAACP, which filed a lawsuit against Harmeet Dhillon, head of the justice department’s civil rights division, who has said she hopes to send 1,000 monitors to midterm polling sites in November. “To be clear, there is no federal statute that gives the US Department of Justice [DoJ] the right to send their so-called ‘election monitors’ to polling sites across the country, and this administration has made clear its intent to disrupt and interfere with the way elections are run in our country,” said Kristen Clarke, the NAACP’s general counsel, who served in Dhillon’s role under Joe Biden. The NAACP lawsuit seeks to force the Trump administration to release records regarding the deployment of the monitors. The NAACP said it requested such records under the Freedom of Information Act on 1 September, but said the Department of Justice had failed to comply.

California: A federal court struck down a California rule that required voters to sign and return vote-by-mail ballots on paper, rather than doing so electronically. The ruling in California Council of the Blind v. Weber, announced October 3, is a victory for disabled voters—but it probably won’t change things much until the next election cycle. Since 2021, California has mailed ballots to all registered voters for all statewide and local elections. Voters with print disabilities—those who are blind or low-vision, or whose physical disabilities prevent them from marking a paper ballot—can request a link to vote electronically. After filling out their ballot on a computer, they must print it, put it in an envelope, seal the envelope, and return it “through non-electronic means.” This is complicated by the fact that many blind people don’t own printers, because they have no use for them. And that’s only one of the obstacles they might face under the current vote-by-mail rules. Christopher Gray, a resident of San Francisco County and a plaintiff in the lawsuit, is blind. Under the current system, as he wrote in a 2024 court declaration, he is unable to vote without assistance. The court ruled that the current vote-by-mail system does exclude some disabled voters, and “denies them meaningful access to the vote by mail program,” as Magistrate Judge Sallie Kim wrote. The court, however, did not specify a resolution. The state of California now has until October 19 to propose a new vote-by-mail protocol, and since California ballots have already been mailed out, any changes made likely will not take effect until the next election cycle.

Florida: US District Judge David Leibowitz dismissed ​a federal case that accused a Jamaican national of illegally voting, finding the law criminalizing noncitizen voting was unconstitutional. The ruling applies only to the case of Florida resident Chelsea Cox, who was charged in October 2025 with illegally voting in the 2020 election. It is not binding for judges in other cases. The ​ruling by Leibowitz, who was nominated by Democratic President Joe Biden, appears to be the first time a federal court has found the ⁠1996 law a violation of the US Constitution. Leibowitz wrote that under the US Constitution only states can set voter qualifications, not Congress. Every state requires US citizenship to vote, and Leibowitz said ​it was up to Florida, not the Department of Justice, to prosecute her for violating that requirement. “Can the State of Florida prohibit and punish the exact conduct Defendant Cox allegedly engaged in ​here? This has already been decided elsewhere and is not addressed here: Florida can and does,” Leibowitz wrote. Cox entered the United States as a tourist in 1999 and remained after her visa expired the next year, according to a court filing by the government. She used a fraudulent US Virgin Islands birth certificate to obtain a Florida driver’s license and used those documents to register to vote in 2012. Records show she voted in federal elections from 2012 to 2020, but only the 2020 election was within the statute of limitations. 

Kansas: The Kansas Court of Appeals has ruled that mail-in ballots arriving three days after the November general election will still count in Kansas. It was the latest ruling in a court battle between Secretary of State Scott Schwab and Kansas Appleseed, along with Kansans who believe a 2025 statute eliminating a three-day grace period for mail-in votes violates their constitutional rights. The statute changed state law to say only advance ballots received by 7 p.m. on Election Day would be counted. “When considering important constitutional challenges concerning election procedure and faced with an upcoming election, courts must be careful to avoid rulings that could cause votes to be lost,” said a ruling from Chief Judge Sarah Warner and Judges Stephen Hill and Kim Schroeder.   Schwab had asked the Kansas Court of Appeals to stay an injunction that forced the state to continue accepting mail-in votes for three days after the election while the court cases proceeded. “Considering the Secretary’s preliminary showing on the merits, the State’s asserted injuries, the irreparable consequences a stay could impose on voters, the public interest, and the rapidly approaching election calendar, we conclude that the Secretary has not met his burden to demonstrate that the temporary injunction should be suspended while this appeal proceeds,” the appeals court ruling said.  The court on Friday agreed to Schwab’s motion to fast-track the appeal but said that still wouldn’t occur on the timetable Schwab requested. He asked the court to hear arguments before Oct. 14, when mail-in ballots would be sent to voters. 

Michigan: Thirty-seven absentee ballots that were found after the 2025 Hamtramck mayoral race will finally be counted, nearly a year after the election took place. Once counted, those could affect who holds the seat. Attorney Mark Brewer, who represents former councilman and mayoral candidate Muhith Mahmood, says the Board of Canvassers has until Oct. 16 to count the ballots. While an order has not been filed, Brewer says it will be done through a public meeting, where the results will be announced that day. The exact date and time of when the count will happen is currently unknown. CBS Detroit has reached out to the Hamtramck city clerk’s office and the Wayne County Board of Canvassers for comment and is awaiting a response. “Nothing like this has taken this long. I really do blame the city and the current mayor. They have been fighting to keep their own people’s votes from being counted for a year,” Brewer told CBS News Detroit.

An Ottawa County woman has been charged for allegedly tampering with her children’s absentee ballots in the August primary election. The Ottawa County Clerk’s Office said the woman had submitted two absentee ballots for her adult children when staff with the Park Township Clerk’s Office noticed discrepancies in the signatures during the signature verification process. Staff also noticed similarities between the woman’s signature and the signatures on her children’s ballot envelopes. Neither of the ballots were counted. The Ottawa County Sheriff’s Office was called in to investigate, and the prosecutor’s office charged the woman with absentee ballot tampering. The woman is expected to be arraigned on Oct. 26. “Our election staff followed the established procedures, recognized that something was not right and acted immediately,” said Park Township Clerk Joan Zeerip. “The safeguards surrounding absentee voting exist to ensure that every ballot is cast by the voter to whom it was issued.”

Montana: Lewis and Clark County District Court Judge Mike Menahan has ruled a law requiring voters to put their birth year on absentee ballots violated the state’s constitution, and has ruled in favor of Disability Rights Montana and Big Sky 55+, which challenged the legislation. Ballots for the Nov. 5 general election have already been sent overseas, with voter packets headed to Montanans by the end of the week. Some overseas ballots have been sent and returned. “Balanced against the state’s lack of evidence of election fraud and its inability to explain how public confidence in elections is elevated by requiring a birth year on absentee ballots, the state has failed to meet its burden of proving HB 719 is reasonable and serves a legitimate state interest or that its asserted interests are more important than the burden on the right to vote,” Menahan wrote in his decision. The court compared voter data from the November 2024 election, which was before the new birth date law, with an all-mail May 5, 2026, election that was held in 30 counties after it was signed into law. Lewis and Clark County’s ballot rejection rate was 0.29% in 2024 and 0.8% in the May 2026 election. In Gallatin County it rose from 0.47% in 2024  to 2.75% in 2026, court documents state.

New Hampshire: U.S. District Court Judge Joseph Laplante, in a 50-page preliminary order, declined to set aside the law Gov. Kelly Ayotte signed in April (HB 323) preventing a student ID from gaining acceptance on its face as all that was needed for someone to obtain a ballot. This law stands for the Nov. 3 election. However, Laplante said Secretary of State David Scanlan went too far with an April directive he sent out to all city and town clerks which said that student IDs were unacceptable to be offered for anyone to register to vote or cast a ballot. “The Legislature decided and has provided that a student ID is no longer, on its own, a expressly valid form of identification,” Laplante wrote. “It does not follow, and the state has not explained why it should follow, that it also decided that a student ID also may not constitute evidence a polling station official may rely on to establish voter identity when no other form of ID is available at all.” Laplante said Scanlan’s directive was too all-encompassing because the Legislature has yet to strike from the books a second state law that lets voters show student IDs or other documents to prove identity if they lack a photo ID. Known as the “catch-all” or safety valve law, it permits a voter to offer “any other evidence” other than a driver’s license, passport or government-issued photo ID if it “reasonably establishes that it is more likely than not that the person is who they claim to be.”

South Carolina: U.S. District Judge Mary Geiger Lewis tossed out a lawsuit from South Carolina civil rights organizations over state absentee voting rules. In the lawsuit, the groups argued a law meant to prevent election fraud makes it harder for people with disabilities to vote. The state NAACP and a trio of disabled nursing home residents filed suit last year challenging limits on assistance with mail-in ballots, arguing the limits violate the federal Voting Rights Act. The 2022 law that created no-excuse-needed early voting set new parameters for voting by mail. People who can’t vote in person during the early voting window or on Election Day, including people with physical disabilities, can request a paper absentee ballot. But to prevent the possibility of fraud, legislators added a cap of five ballots that any one person could request or submit for someone else. Geiger Lewis ruled tthe state NAACP and disabled residents didn’t have grounds to challenge the law. Her order said the three nursing home residents never alleged they’d been denied assistance, only that they could be. “Concern is understandable, but concern is not concreteness. A fear founded on future choices is too fragile for federal jurisdiction,” Geiger Lewis wrote in dismissing the case.

Texas: Fort Worth City Council member Deborah Peoples, in her capacity as a private citizen, is suing Tarrant County’s Republican commissioners and elections administrator, seeking a temporary injunction to halt the approved list of polling sites for the November midterm elections. Peoples filed the lawsuit October 2 with former Judge Maryellen Hicks, former city council member Kathleen Hicks and elected Fort Worth ISD trustee Camille Rodriguez. The lawsuit is against County Judge Tim O’Hare, commissioners Matt Krause and Manny Ramirez, and elections administrator Clint Ludwig. In their petition, the plaintiffs request an expedited evidentiary hearing as the midterms fast approach. Early voting for the Nov. 3 elections is Oct. 19-30.  The lawsuit comes one month after Republican county commissioners approved Ludwig’s recommended list of polling sites for the midterms that eliminated 92 of the sites used in the 2022 midterms. That decision, which the commissioners court’s two Democrats voted against, came after hours of public testimony from residents, voting rights advocates and candidates on the ballot — the vast majority of whom were opposed to the reduction. The lawsuit asks a district court judge to reinstate the 92 sites and require the defendants to pay attorneys’ costs. Plaintiffs are not seeking monetary damages or the cancellation of the election.  

Washington: The U.S. Supreme Court on October 5 set the stage for another redrawing of Washington state’s political maps. On the first day of its new session, the court accepted a legal challenge to the boundaries, then remanded it to a lower court to determine if the way a federal judge drew the lines in 2024 complies with a ruling earlier this year in a Louisiana redistricting case. The action will not affect next month’s election. But it could lead to the state’s legislative district boundaries changing before they’re next scheduled to be redrawn in 2031, depending on the pace at which the process proceeds. Those contesting the 2024 map and the state attorney general had each asked the Supreme Court to take this step.  This case dates back more than four years. It started when a group of Latino voters contested the 15th Legislative District borders adopted by the state’s redistricting commission and approved by the Washington Legislature in early 2022. Their lawsuit contended the final map violated the federal Voting Rights Act because it impaired the ability of Latino voters in the Yakima Valley to participate equally in elections. The case included a trial in June 2022 featuring testimony from commissioners and voting experts.

Wyoming: The Wyoming Supreme Court upheld the state’s crossover-voting ban, ruling that the 96-day period in which voters are prohibited from changing party affiliation ahead of Election Day does not violate their constitutional rights.  “After balancing the right to vote against the legislature’s duty to ‘secure the purity of elections’ and to ‘guard against abuses of the elective franchise,’… we conclude the party affiliation deadline is not an unconstitutionally onerous burden on Appellants’ right to vote and does not violate equal protection guarantees,” the court stated in its unanimous ruling.  The decision clears the way for November’s general election to proceed as it has since 2023, when the Wyoming Legislature adopted a law that prohibits what’s known as crossover voting. Prior to the crossover-voting ban, Wyoming voters could change their party affiliation at the polls on Election Day.
